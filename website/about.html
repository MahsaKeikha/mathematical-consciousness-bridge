About the researcher · Mahsa Keikha, PhD · Connected Care

The question that kept drawing me deeper.

My curiosity about consciousness came long before this repository. What changed over time was not the question itself, but the scientific language I had available to ask it.

Where this research came from

From a persistent curiosity to a problem I wanted mathematics to confront

I have been curious about consciousness for as long as I can remember. But during my doctoral years, when I began research at TRIUMF, Canada's Particle Accelerator Centre and immersed myself more deeply in quantum mechanics, particle accelerators, measurement, and the physical description of nature, that curiosity became a much more serious scientific question.

I began writing a book about consciousness, human experience, physics, and the conceptual questions that arise when we try to understand reality from the language of quantum mechanics. I completed the manuscript. I never published it.

The reason was simple, but important: it never felt finished to me.

I could articulate the questions. I could connect ideas from physics and human experience. I could describe intuitions about consciousness and the limits of description. But I did not yet have a mathematical framework strong enough to tell me which parts could become scientific hypotheses, which could be measured, which could be falsified, and which remained philosophical possibilities.

I did not want elegant language to substitute for evidence.

The missing part

The book was complete as a manuscript, but incomplete as a scientific program

For years I continued to feel that something essential was missing from that early work. The problem was not a missing chapter. It was a missing mathematical layer.

This research program is, in part, my return to those questions with tools I did not have when I first wrote about them. Some concepts that once existed for me only as qualitative questions can now be expressed through formal definitions, identifiability conditions, sufficiency tests, measurement channels, model constraints, finite-data guarantees, counterexamples, and explicit falsification criteria.

That does not mean the old questions have all been answered. It means that some of them can finally be asked in a form that other researchers can inspect, challenge, reproduce, and extend.

The standard I am trying to impose

What exactly is being measured?

What information is preserved or lost?

What is identifiable from the observations?

What assumptions make the conclusion possible?

What observation could make the model fail?

What remains unknown after the mathematics is finished?

From particle physics to human signals

The scientific thread changed scale, but not character

Today my work is much closer to the complexity of living human systems. As founder and CEO of Connected Care, I continue to study questions involving human biomarkers, physiological and behavioral signals, multimodal sensing, aging, neuroscience, cognitive change, and dementia.

At first glance, work on particle accelerators and quantum mechanics may seem far removed from research on aging and human signals. For me, they are connected by a recurring scientific problem:

What can an observable measurement legitimately tell us about an underlying state that is richer than the measurement itself?

A physiological signal is not the person. A movement trajectory is not cognition. A neural measurement is not experience. A behavioral observation is not consciousness. Each is a measurement channel carrying partial information about a system whose full state is not directly available to us.

This becomes especially important in aging and dementia research, where memory, mobility, language, behavior, physiology, sleep, autonomic signals, and environmental context may all change together. More sensors do not automatically produce more understanding. The scientific challenge is to determine which latent distinctions are identifiable, which measurements are informative, which conclusions survive uncertainty, and where the evidence is fundamentally insufficient.

Why Connected Care belongs in this story

Measurement, dignity, and the complexity of human state

Connected Care is where these foundational questions meet applied human-centered research. Studying signals relevant to senior care, neurological change, and dementia continually reinforces the same lesson: the human state cannot be reduced casually to a convenient observable.

The practical work asks how physiological, behavioral, neural, and environmental signals can help us recognize meaningful changes in a person. The mathematical work asks what must be true before any such signal can support a justified inference about an independently specified target.

I see these as two scales of the same research path. One is close to people, sensors, biomarkers, aging, and care. The other is close to probability, physics, identifiability, sufficiency, and falsification. Both are ultimately about the discipline required to move from what we can observe to what we are entitled to conclude.

The question now

Can parts of the consciousness problem be made mathematically testable?

The Mathematical Consciousness Bridge does not begin by assuming that consciousness is quantum, nonphysical, a state of matter, an additional dimension, or any particular latent variable. Its purpose is more demanding: to specify what would have to be measured and preserved before a proposed physical-to-experiential relationship could become a falsifiable scientific claim.

That is why the program has moved repeatedly toward questions of representation, sufficiency, target provenance, measurement channels, latent-state identifiability, model adequacy, finite-sample inference, adaptive analysis, and reproducibility.

In that sense, this project is not a departure from the book I wrote years ago. It is my attempt to return to some of its unfinished questions with mathematics, explicit assumptions, executable tests, and a research record strong enough to be examined by other scientists.

The personal history explains why I care about the question. It is not evidence for any theorem. The scientific claims in this repository stand or fall on their stated assumptions, proofs, computations, tests, external sources, and eventual empirical evaluation.
